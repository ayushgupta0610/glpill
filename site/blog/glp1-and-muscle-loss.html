GLP-1s and Muscle Loss: How to Keep Your Strength While You Lose Weight

Updated July 2026 · 6 min read

When you lose weight on a GLP-1 — pill or injection — not all of what comes off is fat. Some of it is muscle. That's true of almost any fast weight loss, but it's worth understanding, because muscle is what keeps you strong and supports your metabolism, and there are two well-established ways to protect it.

How much of the loss is muscle?

Research on GLP-1 weight loss consistently finds that a meaningful share of the weight lost is lean mass — commonly cited figures land in the range of roughly 20–40% of total weight lost, with many systematic reviews centering around 20–30%. The exact number varies by person, medication, how fast you lose, and — crucially — what you do about it. That last part is the good news: this isn't fixed. The lean-mass share is something you can meaningfully lower.

The reassuring part: muscle loss during weight loss is largely modifiable. The two levers that matter most are getting enough protein and doing resistance training. Used together, they work better than either one alone.

Lever 1: Enough protein

Protein is the raw material your body uses to maintain muscle, and when you're eating less overall (which is the whole point of a GLP-1), protein has to be protected rather than crowded out. Commonly cited evidence-based targets fall in the range of about 1.2–2.0 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day — higher within that range when you're actively losing and training. Some practitioners frame it instead as roughly 1 gram per pound of lean mass. These are general references, not a personal prescription: your right number depends on your body, activity, age, and any conditions (like kidney issues) that change protein needs, so it's worth setting with your prescriber or a registered dietitian.

The practical challenge is real — a smaller appetite makes hitting a protein target genuinely hard. Leading with protein at each meal, front-loading it earlier in the day, and leaning on protein-dense options all help. (We go deeper on this in Protein on GLP-1s.)

Lever 2: Resistance training

Protein gives your body the material; resistance training gives it the reason to keep the muscle. Lifting — or any progressive resistance work — signals your body to hold onto lean mass even in a calorie deficit. Commonly cited guidance is resistance training around 2–3 times per week (some sources suggest up to 3–5 days), focusing on compound movements that work large muscle groups: squats, lunges, rows, push-ups, deadlifts. A typical structure people reference is roughly 8–12 repetitions per set, 2–3 sets per exercise. You don't need a gym or heavy weights to start — bodyweight and resistance bands count, and consistency matters more than intensity at the beginning.

Why the combination wins

The research is fairly consistent on this: protein plus resistance training preserves more muscle (and bone) than diet alone, a high-protein diet alone, or exercise alone. They're complementary — the protein supplies the building blocks, the training tells your body to use them for maintenance rather than let muscle go. On a GLP-1, where you're losing weight relatively efficiently, doing both is how you make sure what you lose is mostly the fat you're trying to lose.

What to keep an eye on

The scale can't tell fat from muscle — it just shows total weight. Signs you're on the right track are less about the number and more about how you feel and function: keeping your strength, energy, and the way clothes fit, rather than feeling weak or frail as the weight comes off. Tracking your protein (at least for a while, until it's intuitive) and staying consistent with training are the levers you actually control. Some people also periodically check body composition, but that's optional; the habits are what matter.
