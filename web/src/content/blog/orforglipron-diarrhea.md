Yes, diarrhea can happen on orforglipron (Foundayo) — it's listed as one of the common gastrointestinal side effects on the FDA-approved label, alongside nausea, vomiting, and constipation. It tends to show up most during dose increases and is usually manageable with simple supportive steps, but persistent or severe cases should go to your prescriber, not to a home remedy.

## Why does orforglipron cause diarrhea?

Orforglipron is a **GLP-1 receptor agonist** — it works by mimicking a gut hormone that slows digestion and affects appetite signaling. That same mechanism, which is central to how the drug works, is also why GI side effects are common across the entire GLP-1 class, not just orforglipron. Slowing digestion changes how your gut processes food and fluid, and for some people that shows up as diarrhea rather than (or alongside) nausea or constipation. If you want the mechanism itself explained in more depth, see [How Does Orforglipron Work?](/blog/how-does-orforglipron-work/)

## How common is diarrhea with orforglipron?

Per the FOUNDAYO label on DailyMed, diarrhea is among the gastrointestinal adverse reactions reported with orforglipron, in the same category as nausea, vomiting, and constipation. The label doesn't single out diarrhea as rare or unusual — it's part of the expected GI side-effect pattern for this drug class, which is also documented for semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy, Rybelsus) on MedlinePlus. That said, individual experience varies widely: some people never notice it, others get it briefly during a dose step-up, and a smaller group finds it persistent enough to need a prescriber conversation.

## Diarrhea vs. constipation on orforglipron — can you get both?

It's a fair question, since they sound contradictory. The FDA label lists both as possible GI side effects of orforglipron, and GLP-1 medications as a class are associated with the same pattern per MedlinePlus. There's no rule that you'll only get one: some people cycle between loose stools and constipation as their gut adjusts, especially during the titration period when the dose is still changing. If constipation is your main issue instead, [Orforglipron and Constipation](/blog/orforglipron-constipation/) covers that side specifically, and [Managing Orforglipron Nausea](/blog/managing-orforglipron-nausea/) covers the other most-reported GI effect.

## What helps manage diarrhea on orforglipron

These are general, commonly discussed supportive steps for GLP-1-related GI side effects — not medical advice, and not a substitute for calling your prescriber if symptoms are frequent, severe, or you notice signs of dehydration:

- **Stay hydrated.** Diarrhea increases fluid loss; replacing fluids (and electrolytes if it's frequent) matters more than any specific food choice.
- **Go easy on very fatty, greasy, or spicy foods** during a flare, since these can be harder to digest while your gut is already adjusting.
- **Eat smaller meals** rather than large ones, which can be easier on a digestive system that's processing food more slowly.
- **Don't self-adjust your dose.** If diarrhea is tied to a recent dose increase, your prescriber — not a self-directed skip or reduction — is the right person to decide whether to slow the titration schedule.

## When to contact your prescriber

Reach out promptly, rather than waiting it out, if you notice:

- Diarrhea that's severe, frequent, or doesn't improve after a few days
- Signs of dehydration — unusual thirst, dark urine, dizziness, or reduced urination
- Diarrhea accompanied by significant abdominal pain, fever, or blood in your stool
- Any GI symptom severe enough that you're considering stopping the medication on your own

None of that is a symptom list to self-diagnose against — it's a prompt to call your prescriber, who can evaluate what's actually going on and whether it's related to orforglipron, dose timing, or something else entirely.

## Bottom line

Diarrhea is a recognized, common GI side effect of orforglipron, in the same category as nausea, vomiting, and constipation, per the FDA-approved FOUNDAYO label. It tends to be most noticeable during dose increases and is often manageable with hydration, smaller and lower-fat meals, and time — but it's not something to self-treat indefinitely if it's severe or persistent. Your prescriber can tell you whether what you're experiencing is typical, whether your titration pace should change, and when a symptom needs more than supportive care.
