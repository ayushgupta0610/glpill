Orforglipron (Foundayo) doesn't directly cause vitamin or nutrient deficiencies, and there's no such deficiency listed on its FDA-approved label. The real concern is indirect: by reducing appetite and sometimes causing nausea or GI upset, orforglipron can lead you to eat less — and eating meaningfully less for weeks or months, without attention to what's in those smaller meals, is what can eventually create a nutrient shortfall. Here's what's actually documented, and what to watch for.

## What the FDA label actually says

Per the FDA-approved FOUNDAYO label on DailyMed, orforglipron's documented effects are appetite reduction and gastrointestinal side effects — nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and constipation are the most common, particularly during dose titration. The label does not list a specific vitamin or mineral deficiency as a direct effect of the drug. That distinction matters: this isn't a medication that blocks nutrient absorption the way certain drugs for other conditions do. The pathway to a deficiency, if one develops, runs through reduced food intake, not a pharmacological mechanism targeting a specific nutrient.

## Why a weight-management drug raises this question at all

Orforglipron is FDA-approved for chronic weight management, and appetite suppression is part of how it works. Semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy) and tirzepatide (Mounjaro, Zepbound) — per their MedlinePlus drug information — share this same general mechanism and the same class-wide GI side effects. Across this drug class, the practical concern clinicians and dietitians raise isn't a drug-specific deficiency, it's the same issue that comes up with any sustained reduction in food intake: if you're eating noticeably less, you need to make what you do eat count, or you risk falling short on protein, fiber, and specific vitamins and minerals over time.

This is the same logic covered in [What to Eat on Orforglipron](/blog/eating-on-orforglipron/) — the emphasis on nutrient-dense food isn't incidental advice, it's the direct answer to the appetite-suppression tradeoff this drug class creates.

## Who's at higher risk

Nobody can tell you from a general article whether you personally are headed toward a deficiency — that depends on your baseline diet, how much your intake has actually dropped, and factors like age, pregnancy status, or existing GI conditions that your prescriber already knows about. That said, situations that typically raise attention in nutrition guidance for reduced intake include:

- A rapid or large drop in how much you're eating, especially early in titration when nausea is most common
- Persistent vomiting or diarrhea lasting more than a day or two, which can affect fluid and electrolyte balance on top of calories
- A diet that's shrunk in portion size but hasn't shifted toward more nutrient-dense choices
- Pre-existing conditions or dietary patterns (certain GI conditions, restrictive diets) that already carried some nutrient risk before starting orforglipron

If any of this describes your situation, it's worth raising directly with your prescriber rather than waiting to see if symptoms show up.

This article isn't going to hand you a list of "take X mg of Y" — that's not something a general article can responsibly do, and doses matter per person. But in general nutrition discussions around reduced food intake, a few categories come up repeatedly:

## Should you take a multivitamin?

Some prescribers suggest a daily multivitamin as a simple, low-risk safeguard for patients on weight-management GLP-1s who are eating substantially less. Others prefer to check labs first and supplement specific gaps rather than take a blanket approach. Both are reasonable clinical approaches — the point is that it's a decision made with your actual diet and bloodwork in view, not something to self-start based on a general guide. Bring it up at your next visit if you haven't already.

## Practical signs worth mentioning to your prescriber

Routine labs your prescriber orders are a far more reliable way to catch a developing deficiency than waiting for symptoms, since several nutrient shortfalls don't produce obvious symptoms until they're well established. Still, it's reasonable to flag: unintentional weight loss happening faster than you and your prescriber expected, fatigue that persists well past the first few weeks of adjustment, or an inability to keep down solid food for more than a day or two. None of these confirm a deficiency on their own, but they're the kind of thing worth mentioning at your next check-in rather than waiting for a scheduled visit.

If GI side effects are the main driver of your reduced intake, [Managing Orforglipron Nausea](/blog/managing-orforglipron-nausea/) and [Orforglipron Constipation](/blog/orforglipron-constipation/) cover the symptom side of this; staying hydrated matters too, which [Orforglipron and Hydration](/blog/orforglipron-and-hydration/) addresses directly.

## Bottom line

Orforglipron's FDA label doesn't list a vitamin or nutrient deficiency as a direct drug effect — the real pathway is indirect, through reduced appetite and GI side effects lowering how much and how well you eat. That's a manageable risk with attention to diet quality, not an inevitability, and it's not something to self-diagnose or self-supplement around. If you're eating noticeably less since starting orforglipron, tell your prescriber so they can decide whether labs, a dietitian referral, or a multivitamin make sense for your specific situation.
