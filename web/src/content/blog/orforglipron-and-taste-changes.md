Yes — orforglipron (Foundayo) can change how food tastes for some people. It's listed on the FDA-approved label as "dysgeusia," an uncommon but real adverse reaction reported in clinical trials. Here's what the label actually says about it, how it compares to orforglipron's more common side effects, and what's reasonable to try if it happens to you.

## What the FDA label actually says

Per the FOUNDAYO (orforglipron) prescribing information on DailyMed, dysgeusia — a medical term covering any change in taste, including a metallic, altered, or diminished sense of taste — is one of the adverse reactions reported in clinical trials. In pooled data from the drug's trials, it occurred in a small percentage of patients, notably more often than in patients taking a placebo:

| Adverse reaction | FOUNDAYO patients | Placebo patients |
|---|---|---|
| Dysgeusia (taste change) | 0.9% | 0.3% |

That's a real, labeled effect — not a rumor — but it's also uncommon. For context, the label lists orforglipron's most frequent adverse reactions (nausea, constipation, diarrhea, vomiting, dyspepsia, abdominal pain, headache, abdominal distension, fatigue, eructation, gastroesophageal reflux, flatulence, and [hair loss](/blog/does-orforglipron-cause-hair-loss/)) as occurring in 5% or more of patients — meaningfully more often than dysgeusia. If you want the full picture of what's common versus rare, [orforglipron's full side effect list](/blog/orforglipron-side-effects/) breaks down the more frequent reactions in detail.

## What does "dysgeusia" (orforglipron metallic taste) actually mean?

Dysgeusia is the clinical term for any disruption to your normal sense of taste — the specific complaint people report most often across GLP-1 medications generally is an orforglipron metallic taste, though it can also show up as food tasting "off," bland, or bitter. The FDA label doesn't break dysgeusia down into these subtypes — it's reported as a single adverse reaction category — so there's no verified breakdown of which specific taste change is most common with orforglipron specifically.

## Why would a GLP-1 pill affect taste?

The FOUNDAYO label doesn't give a specific mechanism for dysgeusia, so this isn't a confirmed drug-specific explanation — but a few general, non-drug-specific factors are worth knowing. GLP-1 receptor agonists as a class slow gastric emptying, which can contribute to reflux for some people, and reflux can leave a lingering taste in the mouth. Appetite and taste perception are also closely linked physiologically, so a medication that changes appetite signaling could plausibly affect how food tastes to some people, too. None of this is a verified mechanism specific to orforglipron — it's general context, not a claim the label makes.

If reflux-type symptoms are part of what you're noticing, [orforglipron and heartburn](/blog/orforglipron-heartburn/) covers that specific side effect in more depth.

## How long does it last?

The FOUNDAYO label doesn't specify a typical duration for dysgeusia, so there's no verified timeline to quote here. Many of orforglipron's gastrointestinal side effects are most noticeable during the early titration period and tend to ease as your body adjusts to a given dose — but that pattern isn't confirmed specifically for taste changes, and individual experience varies. If it hasn't improved after a reasonable period, or it's affecting your eating, that's worth raising with your prescriber rather than guessing at a timeline.

## What might help (general, low-risk habits)

This isn't medical advice, and it isn't a substitute for talking to your prescriber — but a few general habits some people find worth trying if food tastes different:

- **Stay hydrated.** A dry mouth can make taste changes feel more pronounced.
- **Keep up oral hygiene** — brushing, flossing, and (if your dentist is fine with it) a tongue scraper can reduce a lingering off-taste.
- **Switch to non-metal utensils** if you notice a specifically metallic taste; some people find plastic or wooden utensils reduce it.
- **Try cold, tart, or strongly flavored foods** (citrus, mint) if bland food tastes especially "off" — some people find these easier to taste normally.
- **Pay attention to whether it's changing what or how much you eat.** If taste changes are affecting your nutrition, that's worth a conversation with your prescriber, not just a workaround. Our guide to [eating on orforglipron](/blog/eating-on-orforglipron/) has more on managing appetite and food intake generally while titrating.

## When to loop in your prescriber

Reach out to your prescriber if taste changes are persistent, worsening, or affecting your appetite and nutrition — especially alongside other symptoms. They can evaluate whether it's connected to your current dose, whether anything else might explain it, and whether any adjustment makes sense for you. Don't change your own dose or stop orforglipron based on this article; that's a decision for you and your prescriber together.

## Bottom line

Taste change (dysgeusia) is a real, FDA-labeled adverse reaction with orforglipron — reported in 0.9% of clinical trial patients versus 0.3% on placebo — but it's uncommon relative to orforglipron's more frequent gastrointestinal side effects. The label doesn't specify a mechanism or a typical duration, so treat any explanation of "why" as general context rather than a confirmed fact about the drug. If it happens to you, simple habits like hydration and oral hygiene are reasonable to try, and your prescriber is the right person to ask if it persists or starts affecting how you eat.
