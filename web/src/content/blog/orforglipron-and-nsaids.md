Can you take ibuprofen, aspirin, or naproxen while on orforglipron? The FDA-approved FOUNDAYO label doesn't call out a specific NSAID interaction, and occasional NSAID use alongside orforglipron is common in practice. But "no labeled interaction" isn't the same as "nothing to think about" — both NSAIDs and orforglipron can affect your GI tract, and NSAIDs carry their own well-known precautions worth keeping in mind. Here's what's actually known, and what isn't.

## What each does

**Orforglipron** (brand Foundayo) is a once-daily oral **GLP-1 receptor agonist**, FDA-approved for chronic weight management in adults with obesity, or overweight with at least one weight-related condition, alongside diet and physical activity. Per its DailyMed label, it's taken with or without food, with no water restriction, and its most commonly reported side effects are gastrointestinal — nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and constipation, especially while the dose is being titrated upward.

**NSAIDs** (nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs) — ibuprofen, naproxen, and aspirin at pain-relief doses — are a completely different drug class, used for pain, inflammation, and fever. They aren't GLP-1 medications and don't share orforglipron's mechanism. NSAIDs are widely known to carry their own precautions independent of any GLP-1 drug: stomach irritation and ulcer risk with frequent use, and kidney-related caution, particularly with long-term use, dehydration, or existing kidney concerns. Orforglipron's slowed-gastric-emptying effect is a mechanism shared across the GLP-1 class — per MedlinePlus drug information for semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy, Rybelsus) and tirzepatide (Mounjaro, Zepbound) products, which describe the same general GI effects — not something unique to orforglipron alone.

## Is there a labeled interaction?

No specific NSAID interaction is named in the FOUNDAYO label on DailyMed. Orforglipron's labeled interaction concerns center on two things: its effect on gastric emptying (which can potentially affect absorption of other oral medications) and an increased hypoglycemia risk when combined with insulin or insulin secretagogues like sulfonylureas — neither of which is specific to NSAIDs. That absence is informative but not a blanket guarantee; a label not calling out a specific combination means the manufacturer hasn't identified an interaction significant enough to flag, not that zero interaction potential exists. For the fuller interaction picture, see [Orforglipron Drug Interactions](/blog/orforglipron-drug-interactions/).

## Where the real overlap is: GI symptoms, not a pharmacological interaction

The more practical consideration with orforglipron and NSAIDs isn't a drug-drug interaction in the classic sense — it's that both can independently affect your stomach:

| | Orforglipron | NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen, aspirin) |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | GLP-1 receptor agonist; slows gastric emptying | Blocks prostaglandins involved in pain/inflammation |
| Common GI effect | Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, constipation (per label, especially during titration) | Stomach irritation, upset, ulcer risk with frequent use |
| Kidney consideration | Not specifically flagged for NSAID combination | Well-established caution with frequent/long-term use or dehydration |
| Labeled interaction with the other drug | None specific to NSAIDs | Not orforglipron-specific |

Taking an occasional NSAID for a headache while on orforglipron isn't inherently dangerous, but if you're already dealing with nausea or GI discomfort — common in the first weeks or after a dose increase — adding a stomach-irritating pain reliever on top can make things feel worse. If GI symptoms are already a challenge, [Managing Orforglipron Nausea](/blog/managing-orforglipron-nausea/) covers general strategies, and it's worth mentioning to your prescriber before reaching for NSAIDs regularly.

## Dehydration is the underrated part of this combination

During orforglipron's titration period, some people eat and drink less due to appetite changes or GI side effects. NSAIDs' kidney-related caution is well known to be more relevant when someone is dehydrated, since the kidneys are more sensitive to NSAID effects when fluid intake is low. That's less about orforglipron and NSAIDs interacting directly and more about orforglipron's early side effects potentially compounding a risk NSAIDs already carry on their own. Staying hydrated and not reaching for NSAIDs as a daily habit without medical guidance are both reasonable general precautions — not specific to this drug combination, but relevant while you're adjusting to a new medication. See [Your First Week on Orforglipron](/blog/first-week-on-orforglipron/) for what else to expect early on.

## Low-dose aspirin is its own case

If you take daily low-dose aspirin for cardiovascular reasons — as opposed to occasional higher-dose aspirin for pain — that's a different clinical picture than the general NSAID question above. Mention orforglipron to whoever manages your aspirin prescription so it's part of your full medication picture, rather than assuming a pain-focused NSAID discussion automatically covers a cardioprotective aspirin regimen.

## What to bring to your prescriber or pharmacist

- How often you actually use NSAIDs — occasional (a few times a month) is a different conversation than daily use
- Which NSAID and typical dose
- Any history of stomach ulcers, GI bleeding, or kidney issues
- Whether you're taking daily low-dose aspirin for a heart-related reason
- Any GI side effects you've already noticed since starting orforglipron

That level of detail lets a pharmacist or prescriber actually reason about your situation, rather than relying on a general article like this one.

If you rely on NSAIDs regularly, that's worth a direct conversation with your prescriber or pharmacist rather than a decision made from a general guide.

## Bottom line

The FOUNDAYO label doesn't flag a specific interaction between orforglipron and NSAIDs like ibuprofen, naproxen, or aspirin. Occasional use alongside orforglipron is common, but both can affect your GI tract, and NSAIDs carry their own stomach and kidney precautions that matter more if you're using them frequently or are dehydrated during dose titration. If NSAIDs are a regular part of your routine, loop in your prescriber or pharmacist rather than assuming the absence of a labeled interaction means no consideration is needed.
